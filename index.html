Core Principles

The Ten Commandments of AI

Memorable enough to pin to the wall. Practical enough to pull up before you hit send.

I
AI is a draft, not a deliverable.

Every output needs your review, your edits, and your name before it goes anywhere. Read every word. If you wouldn't sign it as-is, it's not done.

II
Use only UJA-approved enterprise AI.

Never use personal or free-tier accounts for UJA work. If it's not on the approved list, it doesn't touch our data.

III
Verify before you trust.

AI invents quotes, stats, dates, and names with total confidence. Treat every specific claim as unverified until you've checked it yourself.

IV
Give AI context — it knows nothing about us.

It doesn't know UJA, our donors, our programs, or our community. The more you tell it, the less generic the output.

V
Be thoughtful about what you put in.

Our platforms are secure, but use good judgment. Don't input what you wouldn't want mishandled. When in doubt, summarize or anonymize.

VI
Write in your voice, not the machine's.

If it reads like a chatbot wrote it, rewrite it. Ban the buzzwords. Your donors and colleagues should hear you, not an algorithm.

VII
A human makes every decision.

AI can organize options, surface patterns, and draft recommendations. The decision — what we fund, who we contact, what we say — is always yours.

VIII
Make it better, not just faster.

The point of saving time is to raise the bar, not lower it. Reinvest the minutes into quality, not just volume.

IX
Iterate — the first draft is rarely the best.

Push back. Ask for alternatives. Say "try again, warmer" or "too long, cut by half." The best results take two or three rounds.

X
Keep learning.

AI changes fast. Try new approaches. Share what works with your team. Stay curious.

Ground Rules

Do's & Don'ts

A quick reference for what AI is great for — and where human judgment must stay in the lead.

✓ The Do's

  • Drafting & editing. First drafts of emails, newsletters, invitations; rewrites into plain language; proofreading.
  • Summarizing. Long meeting notes into action items. Reports into one-page summaries. Survey comments into key themes.
  • Brainstorming. Event themes, program names, campaign taglines, interview questions, social-media angles.
  • Explaining & translating. Plain-language explanations for lay committees; draft translations (always with native-speaker review).
  • Structuring. Turn rough notes into memos, project charters, briefing docs, checklists, and agendas.

✕ The Don'ts

  • Never use unapproved tools. Stick to enterprise Copilot, ChatGPT, and Claude — not personal or free-tier accounts.
  • Never treat AI as the final word. AI "hallucinates" — it invents quotes, stats, citations, and URLs with full confidence.
  • Don't skip review on history & current events. AI is especially unreliable here. Verify against a trusted source first.
  • Never let AI be the decision maker. No screening résumés, ranking donors, deciding grants, or assessing client eligibility.
  • Never skip the review step. Read every word before it goes out. Once sent, your name is on it — not the AI's.

The Art of Prompting

The difference between a useless reply and a brilliant one is almost always the prompt. Four ingredients, six tricks.

Role
Who is AI being?
+
Audience
Who will read this?
+
Goal
What do you need?
+
Guardrail
What to avoid?
🔁

Ask for options, not answers

"Give me 5 versions — different tones." Then pick and combine. Much faster than rewriting once.

🎯

Make it iterate with you

"Shorter." "Warmer." "Less corporate." One-word redirects work. Keep going until it lands.

✏️

Turn AI into your editor

Paste YOUR draft, then ask: "Critique this. What's weak? What could be sharper?"

📋

Ask for the format you want

"Return as a bullet list." "Put it in a table." "Format as an email with a subject line."

❓

Let AI ask YOU questions first

"Before you write, ask me 3 clarifying questions." Forces a better brief. Great for complex tasks.

🔄

Say "start over" when stuck

If the first answer is off, don't try to fix it. "Start over. Try a completely different angle."

Ready-to-Use Prompts

Copy, Paste, Refine

Real prompts for tasks you'll actually face at UJA. Swap the [bracketed] parts and iterate.

First-time donor thank-you

Development
"Draft a 4-sentence thank-you email to a first-time Annual Campaign donor who gave $180. Warm, specific, not corporate. Leave name and campaign team signature blank for me to fill in."
Tip: Adjust the amount and campaign to match the gift.

Follow-up after a donor meeting

Development
"Here are my notes from a coffee with a prospect [paste notes]. Draft a follow-up email that thanks them for the time, references 2 specific things they said, and proposes a next step. Warm but professional. Under 150 words."
Tip: Always verify names, dates, and quoted phrases before sending.

Event social media post

Communications
"Write a 60-word LinkedIn post announcing Walk with Israel 2026. Target audience: Toronto Jewish community, families and young professionals. Tone: energizing, proud, inclusive. End with a registration link placeholder."
Tip: Ask for 3 versions. Pick the best sentence from each and combine.

Newsletter blurb

Communications
"Turn these rough notes [paste] into a 100-word newsletter item. Active voice, specific details, one clear call-to-action. No jargon, no 'excited to announce.'"
Tip: Banning clichés up front saves you a rewrite cycle.

Volunteer recruitment post

Programs
"Draft a warm, inviting volunteer call-out for our senior services program. Audience: adults 30-55 looking for meaningful weekly volunteer work. 75 words. End with how to sign up. Don't use the word 'passionate.'"
Tip: Name what NOT to include — saves wasted drafts.

Speaker introduction

Programs
"I'm introducing [name] at a UJA event. Here's their bio [paste]. Write a 90-second spoken introduction. Warm, specific, ends with an applause line. Avoid listing every credential."
Tip: Always practice out loud — AI writes for the page, not the mouth.

Meeting minutes → action items

Admin
"From these notes [paste], pull out: (1) decisions made, (2) action items with owners and dates, (3) open questions for follow-up. Use short bullets. Flag anything unclear."
Tip: Send to attendees for confirmation, not as the final record.

Board briefing note

Admin
"From these messy notes [paste], draft a one-page board briefing. Structure: (1) Background, (2) What we're recommending, (3) Key risks, (4) Ask of the board. Plain language, no more than 400 words."
Tip: Give AI the structure you want — don't let it invent one.

Focus review across platforms

Copilot App
"Based on my work across all platforms from the last 2 weeks — can you tell me where my focus has been?"
Tip: Great Monday morning check-in to orient your week.

Email triage

Outlook · Copilot
"Review my unread emails from the last 24 hours. Group them into: 1) Needs my executive's decision 2) Needs a response from me 3) Can be delegated 4) FYI only. For each item needing action, explain the ask in one sentence and note any deadline."
Tip: Use this to start your morning — takes 30 seconds to review instead of 30 minutes.

Pre-meeting prep

Outlook Calendar · Copilot
"For my meeting today with [NAME] about [TOPIC], find everything from the last 2 weeks across email, Teams, shared folders, and files that relates to [TOPIC] — even if it's not attached to the meeting."
Tip: Run this 15 minutes before a meeting. Walk in prepared every time.

Track commitments you made today

Outlook · Copilot
"Review my sent emails and Teams messages from today. List any follow-ups or commitments I made so nothing slips."
Tip: Run this at end-of-day. Prevents dropped balls.

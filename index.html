Your first 10 minutes with AI

You don't need a manual. You need a few minutes, a low-stakes task, and permission to experiment. Here is how to get started.

Start here — your first ten minutes
  1. Open Microsoft Copilot. Not sure where to find it? Check out our how-to guide.
  2. Ask it about something you already know. Summarize an email thread you've already read, or a meeting you were in. You'll immediately see how well it understands your work.
  3. Read the reply. Is it right? Helpful? Too long? Too robotic?
  4. Ask it to adjust. "Make it shorter." "Sound warmer." "Try again."
  5. Try a real task. Something low-stakes — a draft email, a meeting agenda.
  6. Always read before sending. AI writes fast, not perfectly.

How to build a strong prompt

The Copilot formula

"Using [these emails / this meeting / this file / the last 7 days], do X, and return it as Y."

Three core ingredients

Every strong Copilot prompt has three core ingredients. Get these three right and you'll cover most of your day-to-day work.

1

Source

Tell it where to look. Emails, Teams, a file, a date range. "Based on my emails from the last 7 days…" This is what makes Copilot different from a blank chat window — leave it out and it guesses.

2

Goal

Tell it what to do. Summarize, draft, analyze, compare, extract. Be specific about length and tone while you're at it — "draft a short, warm thank-you."

3

Format

Tell it how to return the answer. Bullets, a table, a one-pager, labelled sections. If you don't say, you'll get paragraphs.

Going further: three more ingredients

When the stakes are higher — a donor email, a board document, anything going outside UJA — three more ingredients are worth adding.

4

Audience

Tell it who will read this. "For our major donors." "For lay-committee volunteers." "For staff who've never used this system." Without it, Copilot writes for everyone, so it lands with no one.

5

Guardrail

Tell it what to avoid. "Don't invent names, dates or dollar figures." "No jargon." "Under 100 words." Usually the fastest way to fix an output you didn't like the first time.

6

Role

Tell it who to write as — but only if that isn't you. Copilot already assumes it's writing for you, in your voice. Add this only when writing on someone else's behalf: "You're drafting this as our CEO."

Weak vs. strong — a worked example

Weak prompt

"Write me an event description."

Why it fails

  • No source — so it invents the event.
  • No audience — it writes for everyone, so it lands with no one.
  • No tone or length — you'll get corporate-flavoured mush.
Strong prompt

"Using the attached NextGen team mandate document, write a 75-word description for a Thursday evening networking event for Jewish young professionals working in tech and startups, Dec 5 at 6 PM. Casual and engaging. Kosher appetizers and complimentary drinks. Don't invent any details that aren't in the document. End with an RSVP call-to-action."

What makes it work

  • Source named, so it works from real content instead of guessing.
  • Specific audience — not just "young professionals," but a defined group.
  • Tone set, length capped, guardrail against invented detail.
  • Clear ending — Copilot knows where to land.

Six tricks to level up

Ask for options, not answers

"Give me 3 versions — different tones." Then pick and combine. Much faster than rewriting a single draft over and over.

Turn Copilot into your editor

Paste your own draft, then ask: "Critique this. What's weak? What could be sharper?" One of the best uses of AI — and it never touches your voice.

Let Copilot ask you questions first

"Before you write anything, ask me 3 clarifying questions." Forces a better brief, and saves rounds of iteration on complex tasks.

Show it what "good" looks like

If the output sounds robotic, paste something you liked and say "match this tone." Examples work far better than explanations.

Correct it as you go

When Copilot gets something wrong, tell it — it picks up your preferences as you go. The more you refine, the better it gets.

Iterate, don't restart

"Cut the second paragraph." "Make it less formal." "Same thing, but for a board audience." Refining is almost always faster than starting again — but if the whole angle is wrong, don't polish it: say "start over and try a completely different approach."

Customize your Copilot

The move most first-timers miss. Fill in your profile once, and every conversation starts with context baked in — less typing, better answers.

Why it saves you time

Copilot lets you tell it who you are, who you write for, and how you sound. Fill it in, and every conversation after that starts with the context already baked in. You stop repeating yourself in every prompt, and Copilot stops defaulting to a generic corporate voice. Ten minutes of setup saves hours over the year.

Where to find it

In Copilot this is called custom instructions. To get there:

Open the Microsoft 365 Copilot app → select the "…" menu at the top right → Chat settings → Personalization → Custom instructions.

The templateFive things worth telling Copilot
  1. About me. Your role, team, and what you work on day-to-day.
  2. Who I write for. Your most common audiences — donors, lay committees, agency partners, board, staff.
  3. My voice. Tone preferences. Words to favour, words to ban. Canadian English. Any clichés you never want to see.
  4. UJA context. Programs you reference often, partner agencies, community specifics Copilot wouldn't otherwise know.
  5. My defaults. How you want Copilot to respond: options vs. a single answer, default length, whether to ask clarifying questions first, flag anything invented.
A filled-in exampleA Senior Development Officer at UJA
  1. About me. "I'm a Senior Development Officer at UJA Federation of Greater Toronto. I manage relationships with about 80 major gift donors. My daily work is thank-yous, meeting follow-ups, stewardship updates, and solicitation proposals."
  2. Who I write for. "Individual major donors, family foundations, and their professional advisors — lawyers and accountants."
  3. My voice. "Warm but confident. Specific over generic. Canadian English. Never use: excited, thrilled, partnership, leverage, empower, or 'your investment in our mission.' Short sentences."
  4. UJA context. "I work across the Annual Campaign and planned giving. Our agencies include JF&CS, Baycrest, and Bernard Betel. Never invent UJA facts — ask me if unsure."
  5. My defaults. "Give me 2–3 versions, 150–200 words each. Ask clarifying questions first for complex tasks. Flag anything you're unsure about."

Keep it fresh. Revisit your custom instructions every few months. Add anything you keep having to re-explain in prompts, and remove anything that's no longer relevant. It's also worth sharing your setup with your team — a good one is worth passing around.

Model switching

First — what is an AI model?

An AI model is the engine underneath an AI tool — the part that actually reads your request and writes the answer. Different companies build their own models, and you likely already know them by the name of the app they sit behind: OpenAI builds the GPT models (the technology behind ChatGPT), and Anthropic builds the Claude models. Each one is built and trained a little differently, which is why they don't all answer the same way — one may be a stronger writer, another better at working through a spreadsheet or a long, messy document.

What model switching means in Copilot

Copilot isn't tied to a single AI model. Microsoft has built it so that both OpenAI's GPT models and Anthropic's Claude models run inside Copilot — and you get to choose which one answers you. That's model switching: same Copilot, same access to your work, different engine behind the answer. You don't need a separate ChatGPT or Claude subscription to use them.

In the Copilot app — and in Word, Excel and PowerPoint — you can click the model selector in the top-right corner (usually listed as "Auto") and choose between Anthropic's Claude models and OpenAI's GPT models. Each one has its own strengths, and they're changing and improving almost weekly.

Note that in some apps, the model switcher shows up within the three dots (…) menu.

AI tools are changing quickly, so screenshots or training images may become outdated at any time. If you can't find something in Copilot, or notice that something looks different from the screenshots, please email ai@ujafed.org so we can update the materials.

Which model, when?

Claude Sonnet — the everyday workhorse. Fast, and strong on writing, editing, summarizing and straightforward analysis. A good choice when you want quality without a wait.

Claude Opus — the heavy lifter. Use it for complex, multi-step work: long or messy documents, detailed spreadsheet analysis, building a deck from scratch, or thinking through a strategy or plan. It takes longer, and it's usually worth it.

GPT (OpenAI) — the model family behind ChatGPT, and Copilot's default. The standard version is a strong all-rounder and is good at pulling across your emails, Teams messages, files and calendar. Higher "thinking" versions trade speed for deeper reasoning on harder problems.

Try them all — then pick your favourite per task

There is no single "best" model. Each one has its own strengths and weaknesses, and they shift as the models are updated. The most useful habit to start off is to run the same prompt through more than one model and compare — you'll often be surprised at the difference. Even if you've settled into one you like, it's worth switching now and then to see what the others do with the same ask.

Same menu, second choice: how hard Copilot thinks

In the same selector you'll also see three response options:

  • Auto (default) — Copilot decides how much thinking your prompt needs. Fine for most day-to-day work.
  • Quick Response — answers right away. Best for short, simple asks: a rewrite, a quick definition, a fast draft.
  • Think Deeper — takes longer and reasons through the problem before answering. Best for analysis, comparing options, or anything multi-step where accuracy matters.

Use this section of the menu if you don't want to select based on model, but based on type of task. We do recommend choosing models according to type of task, as laid out in "Which model, when?" above, but this is also an option.

Important information

A note on confidential and HR information

Copilot can only see what you already have access to. It will never open a file, email or message that you couldn't already open yourself — and it is not a way around permissions you don't have.

That also means Copilot should not be used to go looking for confidential information. Questions like "who was let go last month?" — or anything touching personnel files, performance and discipline matters, compensation, leaves, accommodations or health information — are not appropriate Copilot prompts, even out of curiosity. If a question can only be answered with confidential HR information, it belongs with HR, not with Copilot.

If something confidential does surface because you have legitimate access to it, the usual rules still apply: don't forward it, paste it, or re-save it somewhere new.

⚠ Where you save a file matters more than ever

Don't save anything confidential to a Team SharePoint site if you wouldn't want everyone on that site to read it. Those colleagues could always open those files — that hasn't changed. What has changed is how easy they are to find. Copilot surfaces content people may never have known to look for.

Keep confidential material in your personal OneDrive, or in a dedicated SharePoint site with limited, intentional access.

Quick gut check before you save: would I be comfortable with anyone who has access to this location reading this document? If not, it belongs somewhere else.

If you notice confidential content surfacing by accident because it was saved by someone in the wrong place, let the AI team know at ai@ujafed.org. We will point you in the right direction, whether that is IT, Legal or HR, depending on the circumstances, so it can be corrected.

When Copilot isn't working

Like any online tool, Copilot will sometimes be slow, stop partway through an answer, or not respond at all. It isn't something you did, and it usually doesn't last long.

  • Wait a few minutes and try again, or start a new chat.
  • If one model isn't responding, switch to another in the model selector.
  • Still having trouble? Email helpdesk@ujafed.org.

If it's time-sensitive, don't wait on it. Do the task the way you normally would.

Give it time

The most common reaction in the first week is excitement, followed quickly by frustration. That's normal, and it isn't a sign the tool doesn't work. Copilot gets meaningfully better the longer you use it — as it learns how you write, what you care about, and how you like things returned to you. Give it a few weeks before you decide what you think of it.

If you've never used an AI tool before

You don't need any background to start. Begin with one small, low-stakes task — summarizing a long email thread, tidying up a draft — and build from there. Your first few prompts will feel clumsy. Everyone's do. Keep going.

If you've used other AI tools before

Expect an adjustment period. Copilot is built around your work rather than a blank chat window, so the rhythm is different and the same prompt may land differently at first. Give it the same runway you gave whatever you used before.

Think of it as a brand-new colleague who has already read everything

On day one, Copilot doesn't know you — your tone, your priorities, how you like a summary structured. That part takes time. But it isn't starting from nothing: it already has context on your emails, files, meetings and Teams conversations. So it starts off knowing a great deal about the work, and very little about you. The gap closes the more you use it.

What patience actually looks like
  • Use it daily for small things, not just occasionally for big things. Consistency is what builds the learning curve — yours and its.
  • When an answer misses, tell it why instead of walking away. A weak first response is usually a prompt problem, not a dead end.
  • Remember that the models themselves are improving constantly. Something that didn't work well a month ago is worth trying again.

And if you're stuck, ask. Nobody is expected to figure this out alone — reach out at ai@ujafed.org.

UJA AI Code of Conduct

Purpose

Artificial intelligence can help employees draft, summarize, research, organize information and develop ideas. This Code explains the basic standards for using AI responsibly in UJA work.

This Code applies whenever an employee uses an AI tool for UJA work. It is a starting point and will be updated as AI technology and UJA's practices develop.

The core rule

Use AI only for legitimate UJA work and in a way that is consistent with your role, UJA policies and UJA's mission.

Our commitments when using AI

1

We use approved tools

Do
  • Only use AI tools through the enterprise accounts and licenses that UJA has approved and provided for work.
  • "Enterprise" accounts are the special work versions of these tools that UJA pays for and sets up for staff. They have extra privacy and security protections and are separate from the free or personal versions available to the public.
  • Check the Application Portfolio on the Loop to see which tools are approved.
  • Submit any new tools for review and wait for a UJA-provisioned account or license before using or connecting them.
Do not
  • Use personal, free-tier or unapproved AI accounts for UJA work.Why: These tools may not meet UJA's privacy, security or accountability standards.
  • Connect third-party AI tools, plug-ins, bots or agents to UJA systems, accounts or data without approval.Why: Unapproved connections can expose information or give outside tools access they should not have.
2

We protect information

Do
  • Remember that what you can share depends on both the tool and the type of information. UJA-approved does not automatically mean that every kind of information may be entered.
  • Use Copilot for UJA work involving internal and confidential information. Copilot is already connected to our internal files and keeps that information inside UJA's environment.
  • Anonymize, summarize or remove identifying details first if you need to use another platform for that kind of work.
  • Check with your manager or ai@ujafed.org if you are unsure.
Do not
  • Upload sensitive donor data, personnel information, legal files, financial data, or anything else highly confidential to Claude, ChatGPT or any other platform outside UJA's environment.Why: Information entered into outside platforms may leave UJA's controlled environment and may be harder to protect, retrieve or delete.
  • Enter passwords, credentials, banking or credit-card details, unpublished donor lists, HR case files or material covered by a confidentiality agreement.Why: These items create the highest privacy, security and legal risk if exposed.
  • Assume information is appropriate to use simply because you can access it.Why: Access does not always mean permission to share, upload or analyze the information.
3

We review and take responsibility

Do
  • Treat AI-assisted work as a draft. Read, edit and verify it before it is used, sent, published or relied upon.
  • Check names, numbers, dates, quotes, facts, links and sources against the original information, and confirm that the final work does not misuse copyrighted or private material.
  • Make sure the final work is accurate, appropriate and in your own voice.
Do not
  • Assume an answer is correct because it sounds confident or polished.Why: AI can produce inaccurate or incomplete information, so employees remain responsible for checking the work.
  • Submit, send or publish AI-generated content that you have not fully reviewed.Why: Unreviewed content can create errors, reputational risk or unintended commitments on behalf of UJA.
  • Use AI as the only source for important business information.Why: Important decisions should be based on reliable sources, not a single AI-generated response.
4

We keep decisions human-led

Do
  • Use AI to organize information, identify options and draft recommendations.
  • Apply professional judgment and ensure an authorized person makes the final decision.
Do not
  • Allow AI to make a final decision.Why: AI should support human judgment, not replace the accountability of the person or team responsible for the outcome.
  • Use AI as the sole basis for hiring, termination, program participation, grant awards, donor relationship decisions or anything else that has a real impact on a person or on UJA.Why: These decisions require context, judgment and approval by the appropriate human decision-maker.
  • Use AI as a substitute for legal, HR, financial or other professional advice.Why: Professional advice must come from the appropriate qualified person or department.
5

We use AI fairly and honestly

Do
  • Review AI-assisted work for bias, stereotypes, unsupported assumptions and unfair outcomes.
  • Use AI-generated images, audio, video or text only for legitimate, approved purposes.
Do not
  • Use AI in a way that treats people unfairly or unequally. This includes any decision or content that discriminates against someone because of who they are, such as their race, religion, gender, age or disability.Why: AI learns from human data, so it can pick up and repeat unfair biases.
  • Use AI to create fake or misleading content or to pretend to be someone else, such as a staff member, donor, community leader or any other person.Why: Pretending to be someone, or faking their words or image, breaks people's trust and can lead to ethical, legal and reputational problems.
  • Use fabricated content to mislead, manipulate or spread misinformation.Why: UJA communications must be honest, accurate and consistent with our responsibilities to the community.
6

We speak up when something goes wrong

Do
  • Promptly report if something goes wrong or almost goes wrong when using AI. For example:
    • a suspected data breach (such as confidential files being exposed or accessed without authorization);
    • information being shared where it should not have been (such as pasting donor details into an unapproved tool);
    • AI output causing a problem (such as an error in a published document or an email sent based on inaccurate AI-generated information); or
    • a near miss (such as almost uploading a sensitive file before catching the mistake).
  • Report to your manager, IT, Legal or the designated AI team and cooperate in addressing the issue.
  • Ask for help. Early reporting is encouraged so issues can be corrected quickly.
Do not
  • Hide a mistake or continue using a tool or process after identifying a serious concern.Why: Early reporting helps UJA limit harm, correct the issue and prevent it from happening again.
  • Assume someone else will report the issue.Why: If you notice a problem, you are responsible for raising it so the right people can respond.

What Copilot is, how to start, and what to expect. Microsoft Copilot is now available to every staff member at UJA. This page answers the questions we hear most often. Select a question to open the answer.

What is Copilot and what does it actually do?

Copilot is an AI assistant you interact with in plain English, built right into the Microsoft 365 tools you already use every day (Outlook, Teams, Word, Excel, PowerPoint and SharePoint). There is nothing to install and no new platform to learn.

You can ask it to draft an email, summarize a long thread, prep you for a meeting, turn rough notes into a polished document, brainstorm ideas, or analyze data, all inside the apps you already have open. It handles the first pass. You do the rest.

Do I need to know what I am doing first?

No. Open it and ask it something in plain English, the way you would ask a colleague. The Start Here guide on the UJA AI Resource Hub has examples, tips and tricks, and lots of other training content if you want a running start.

One of the best things about AI is that it can teach you how to use it. Open Copilot and ask it for prompt ideas, or for examples of what it could help you with in your role. And you are not doing this alone: come to a drop-in session or email ai@ujafed.org. We are here to help.

This isn't working and I'm frustrated. What do I do?

Give it time. The most common reaction in the first week is excitement, followed quickly by frustration. That's normal, and it isn't a sign the tool doesn't work. Copilot gets meaningfully better the longer you use it, as it learns how you write, what you care about, and how you like things returned to you. Give it a few weeks before you decide what you think of it.

And when the answers feel weak or plain wrong, it almost always comes down to the prompt rather than the tool. Tell it where to look (these emails, this file, the last 7 days), what to do (summarize, draft, compare), and how you want the answer back (bullets, a table, a one-pager). The Copilot prompt guide has the formula and ready-made examples for real UJA tasks, and it is the fastest way to go from disappointing answers to genuinely useful ones.

And be patient:

  • Use it daily for small things, not just occasionally for big things. Consistency is what builds the learning curve — yours and its.
  • When an answer misses, tell it why instead of walking away: "make it shorter," "sound warmer," "try again but add…" A weak first response is usually a prompt problem, and will get better over time.
  • Remember that the models themselves are improving constantly. Something that didn't work well a month ago is worth trying again.
Who is responsible if Copilot gets something wrong?

You are. This is one of the most important things to be aware of. Copilot is a drafting tool, not a source of truth, and it can be confidently wrong: inventing a statistic, misreading a document, or summarizing a meeting in a way that misses the point entirely. It does not know when it is wrong, so it will never flag it for you.

If your name is on it, you own it. Read every draft before you send it, check any number, date, name or quote against the original source, and never forward something you have not actually read. The care matters most on anything that leaves UJA or carries our name: donor communications, board and committee materials, grant applications, anything public. Copilot handles the first pass; the judgment stays with you.

And be careful not to lean on AI too heavily. The risk is not that Copilot writes badly, it is that a good-enough first draft gets sent as finished work. Unedited AI writing and work content is easier to spot than people think: generic, oddly formal, padded with filler, and it does not sound like you. Treat every draft as a starting point, never a final product. Read it, fix it, cut what is not true, and put it in your own voice. Review everything before it goes out. Every time.

I've never used AI before. Will I actually catch on?

You don't need any background to start. Begin with one small, low-stakes task — summarizing a long email thread, tidying up a draft — and build from there. Your first few prompts will feel clumsy. Everyone's do. Keep going.

If you've used other AI tools before, expect an adjustment period: Copilot is built around your work rather than a blank chat window, so the rhythm is different and the same prompt may land differently at first. Give it the same runway you gave whatever you used before.

Think of it as a brand-new colleague who has already read everything. On day one, Copilot doesn't know you, your tone, your priorities, how you like a summary structured. But it isn't starting from nothing: it already has context on your emails, files, meetings and Teams conversations. So it starts off knowing a great deal about the work, and very little about you. The gap closes the more you use it.

What if I want ChatGPT or Claude instead of Copilot? Why Copilot?

You are not giving them up by choosing Copilot. Copilot now lets you switch models inside the same window, so you can point a task at an OpenAI model or an Anthropic Claude model depending on what you are doing, without leaving the tool or opening a second tab. The model choice that used to require a separate subscription increasingly lives inside Copilot itself. (See model switching for how to do it.)

The bigger reason for using Copilot is what sits underneath it. Through Microsoft's Work IQ layer, Copilot is grounded in your actual work: your emails, files, meetings, Teams chats, and the people you work with. It knows a ton about UJA, your files, and last week's meeting. That is why Copilot is our main AI platform: it is where the work already lives, it meets our security and privacy obligations, and it lets us train and support everyone in one place instead of a different tool for every team. Claude and ChatGPT Enterprise may still have a role for specific teams, but Copilot is the baseline for all of us.

What about my existing ChatGPT or Claude license?

If you already have a ChatGPT or Claude license through UJA, keep it for now and we will be in touch, and we recommend you start using Copilot too. If you do not have ChatGPT or Claude, Copilot is where to start.

What if I find a new AI tool that would really help my team?

Bring it forward. We want people experimenting and noticing where AI could genuinely help — that is exactly the kind of thinking this rollout is meant to encourage. Email ai@ujafed.org with the details: what the tool is, the problem you are trying to solve, who would use it, and roughly what it costs.

From there, the AI Ops team reviews it alongside your department lead, IT and Legal. We look at whether Copilot already does it, security and privacy requirements, legal and vendor terms, cost and your department's budget, and how the tool would be supported, following the same procurement process we use for any other software. None of this is meant to slow you down or default to no. It is how we make sure anything we add is safe, necessary, supportable, and worth the spend.

Is my information safe?

Yes. Copilot runs within UJA's enterprise Microsoft 365 environment. Your data is not sent to the public internet, is not used to train AI models, and Copilot only surfaces content you already have permission to access. It meets our legal and privacy obligations, including PIPEDA. Use only the enterprise AI platforms UJA provides for work, and do not use personal or free accounts for UJA work.

What can't I put into Copilot?

Secure does not mean anything goes, and your judgment still matters. Copilot checks whether you have permission to open a file, but it does not understand how sensitive the information inside it is. It will not stop you, warn you, or ask why you need it. That part is entirely up to you.

A good rule of thumb: if the information is about an identifiable person and/or it is not yours to share, do not put it into Copilot. Use real care with donor records, personnel and HR matters, health information, and privileged legal material.

Those are examples, not a complete list. For anything else you are unsure about, ask your manager or email the AI Ops team at ai@ujafed.org before you paste it in. And use your judgment; if something feels like it should not go in, that instinct is usually right.

Why can't I use my personal or free accounts for UJA work?

Because none of the protections above apply to them. A free or personal account sits outside UJA's environment, which means anything you paste into it — donor details, staff information, board materials, draft strategy — leaves our control entirely and may be used to train public AI models. It also creates real privacy and record-keeping problems for us, since that work no longer lives anywhere UJA can access, secure or produce if asked.

The good news is that the version we provide is the better tool anyway. Your UJA Copilot is connected to your emails, files, meetings and Teams chats, so it can actually help with your work instead of starting from a blank window every time.

Can I record or transcribe my meetings — and do I need consent?

Copilot cannot recap a Teams meeting unless recording or transcription is turned on, and that only happens when someone chooses to turn it on. It is not listening in the background. The same expectations apply to any tool that captures a conversation (Teams, a note-taker app, a voice recorder, or your phone sitting on the table).

Ask, don't tell. Before you start, ask the people in the meeting whether they are comfortable being recorded or transcribed, and say what you will use it for. An automatic notification is not consent, and neither is announcing it as a decision already made; people need a genuine opportunity to say no. If anyone is uncomfortable, don't record; take notes instead.

And if something confidential comes up partway through, stop the recording. Say so out loud, pause or end it, and pick it up afterwards if you need to. Some conversations are better left uncaptured altogether — performance and personnel matters, legal discussions, donor conversations, and anything sensitive — because a transcript is a record that can be shared, forwarded or produced later. Transcripts are also only as accurate as the audio, so treat any recap as a draft and correct it before you circulate it.

Will there be training, and who runs it?

Yes. Training will be delivered internally by the AI Ops Team and the AI Leads, tailored to UJA's workflows. In the meantime, the AI Resource Hub has a Start Guide, a Copilot Prompt Guide and Training Materials, the UJA AI Code of Conduct, an FAQ, and Myths of AI.

The bigger picture

Is Copilot the whole plan, or just the beginning?

Copilot is just the first step in a multi-year AI journey and a genuine change in how we work. Launching Copilot puts real capability in everyone's hands from day one, and it is deliberately the starting point that everything else builds on. There is a clear plan behind it, and we are taking it together.

What are the phases of AI at UJA?

We are taking a phased approach so the change is manageable and staff are supported at every step:

  • Phase 1 (mid 2026) — Adoption and capability building. Get Copilot into everyone's hands, build familiarity and confidence, and identify practical use cases. Includes the AI Resource Hub, training, the AI Leads program, and drop-in support sessions in the first months after launch.
  • Phase 2 (late 2026–2027) — Focus and expansion. Identify the highest value use cases across UJA and begin activating our data foundation so AI can support personalization, donor insight and stewardship. Departments test ideas through a light-touch test, learn and scale model, and we begin piloting AI agents in a few selected workflows.
  • Phase 3 (2027 and beyond) — Evolve and adapt. Expand use cases and continue to evolve UJA's approach as AI capabilities advance, staying aligned with our 2026–2030 Strategic Plan and organizational priorities.
I keep hearing about "AI agents." What are they?

An AI agent is a capable assistant that can handle a sequence of tasks from start to finish on your behalf, rather than just answering a single question. They are part of a later phase, not this launch. Before any agents are deployed, UJA will adopt a formal AI Governance Framework and pilot them in a small number of workflows within human-supervised parameters. Even then, your role shifts from doing to reviewing, authorizing and overseeing, so people stay firmly in control.

Will I be left on my own to figure this out?

No. There is a plan here, and the point is to hold your hand through it, not hand you software and walk away. In the first months after launch you will have the AI Resource Hub, an always-on support channel, drop-in support sessions, and AI Leads across the organization to help with real workflows and questions. The first six months are specifically about helping everyone understand and get comfortable with AI.

Will there be ongoing sessions to keep learning?

Yes. Beyond the initial launch and training content, we are planning recurring AI "show and tell" sessions where teams demonstrate how they are actually using AI and share what they have learned. Team meetings will follow the launch with deeper, practical use cases once people have had a chance to experiment, and AI use will be woven into UJA's broader professional development over time. This is about building AI fluency across the organization, not a single event.

Myths of AI

Separating what is true from what is not, as AI arrives at UJA. A lot of chatter comes with it. Some of it is true, some of it is not. This page tackles the myths we hear most often so people can start from informed practice rather than fear or guesswork. Our role is to bring structure to what is already happening. Select a myth to open the answer.

Myth 1: "AI is unreliable and just makes things up."

The reality
There is truth in this one. AI can sound completely confident and still be wrong, even when it has been given good context. Better context and prompting help, such as telling it not to guess and asking where the information came from, but verification is still essential. The opposite belief, that AI is always accurate, is just as risky. It is genuinely useful when you direct it and verify it, and it will let you down if you use it blindly.

What this means for you
Treat every output as a first draft, never a finished product. Check the facts, names, numbers and quotes before you rely on them. Read every word before you send, post or submit. Once it leaves your desk, your name is on it, not the AI's.

Myth 2: "Anything I type into AI becomes public."

The reality
Not in the tools UJA provides. Copilot is the enterprise version, which means your work stays within UJA's secure Microsoft environment, not on the public internet. Our enterprise agreements ensure UJA's data is not used to train public AI models and information remains within our licensed environment. Free AI tools do not offer the same protections, and some may use what you type to improve their models, depending on their settings and terms. That is exactly why we provide enterprise tools.

What this means for you
This is precisely why we ask you to stay inside the tools UJA provides and not use free or personal accounts for UJA work. It also does not mean anything goes. Be thoughtful about what you put in. Highly sensitive material, including personal health information, donor financial details, HR and employee files, and legal matters, still calls for judgment before you share it with any tool. Ask yourself whether the information needs to be there for you to get a useful answer, and leave out what does not.

Myth 3: "AI is going to take my job."

The reality
AI does not replace your judgment, relationships or knowledge. It can help with routine tasks, accelerate a first draft, and reduce administrative work, but people are still responsible for decisions, oversight, creativity and human connection. The most effective use of AI happens when people combine the tool's capabilities with their own experience and judgment.

What this means for you
Think of AI as a tool that supports your work, not a replacement for it. It can help you spend less time on repetitive tasks and more time on the work that requires insight, collaboration and decision-making. The value comes from the judgment, context and expertise you bring.

Myth 4: "Copilot can see everyone's files."

The reality
Copilot only surfaces content you already have permission to access. It respects existing SharePoint, OneDrive and file permissions and does not give anyone visibility into documents they could not already open. It responds using data the user already has permission to view.

What this means for you
Copilot does not change your access. It changes how easily you can find what you already have access to. It works within the access you already have, so you can use it with confidence on the content in front of you.

Important: where you save a file matters more than ever. Do not save anything confidential to a Team SharePoint site if you would not want everyone on that site to read it. Those colleagues could always open those files. That has not changed. What has changed is how easy they are to find, because Copilot surfaces content people may never have known to look for. Keep confidential material in your personal OneDrive, or in a dedicated SharePoint site with limited, intentional access.

A quick gut check before you save: would I be comfortable with anyone who has access to this location reading this document? If not, it belongs somewhere else. If you notice confidential content surfacing by accident because it was saved in the wrong place, let the AI team know at ai@ujafed.org. We will point you in the right direction, whether that is IT, Legal or HR, depending on the circumstances, so it can be corrected.

Myth 5: "AI is the same as Google."

The reality
They are not the same. Google primarily helps you find information by pointing you to pages. AI can synthesize, draft, compare, organize, analyze, and work iteratively with you in a back-and-forth conversation. You can ask it to summarize, rewrite or challenge an idea, then refine the answer by telling it what to change, which is not something a search engine does.

What this means for you
Treat it like a thought partner, not a search box. The more context you give it and the more you iterate, the more useful it becomes.

Myth 6: "I'm not technical enough, and I'm already too far behind."

The reality
You do not need to be technical, and you are not behind. Everyone is learning. If you can write an email, you can use AI. It is conversational, not technical: you type a request in plain English, the way you would ask a colleague, and refine from there. The tools change month to month, so all of us are learning in real time.

What this means for you
There is no pressure to be an expert on day one, and nothing to catch up on before you begin. Start with a low-stakes task, see what comes back, and adjust. The AI Resource Hub has examples if you want a running start, and support, training and colleagues are all there to help.

Myth 7: "Using AI is cheating, and people will think I didn't do the work."

The reality
At UJA, it is the opposite. Using AI well is encouraged, and we want everyone to feel comfortable experimenting, whether you are just getting started or already using it every day. Using it well is the work: the judgment, the editing and the context you bring are exactly what make the output good. A polished output still depends on you knowing what to ask for, what to keep, what to fix, and what to throw out. At UJA we celebrate good use of AI, we do not hide it.

What this means for you
You do not need to hide or downplay that you used AI. It handles the time-consuming first pass so you can focus on the thinking that matters. Owning how you used it, and the thinking you added on top, is the professional standard here.

Myth 8: "AI is only useful for writing emails and documents."

The reality
Writing is a great place to start, but it is only a small part of what AI can do. It can analyze information, compare options, brainstorm ideas, organize messy notes, prepare you for meetings, identify patterns, and challenge your thinking.

What this means for you
Think beyond the draft. The next time you are stuck on a problem, planning a project, or getting ready for an important conversation, ask AI to help you think it through. Some of the most valuable uses are the ones you might not expect.

Myth 9: "If the first answer isn't good, AI isn't very useful."

The reality
The first answer is often just the beginning. AI works best as a conversation, not a one-shot request, and the strongest results usually come after a few rounds of back and forth.

What this means for you
Push back, add context, tell it what it got wrong, and try again. Treat it the way you would a colleague's first draft: give feedback and ask for another version.

Myth 10: "AI will save me time every time I use it."

The reality
Not always, and that is okay. Sometimes it will save you an hour. Sometimes you will spend ten minutes getting it to do something you could have done yourself in five. Time is not the only measure of value, either. The payoff can also be better thinking, a different perspective, or a stronger idea.

What this means for you
Go in with realistic expectations. With practice, you will get a better sense of where AI helps most and where it is quicker to do it yourself. Both are worth learning.

Myth 11: "The safest way to use AI is not to use it."

The reality
Avoiding AI is not the same as using it safely. We do not want anyone so worried about getting AI wrong that they avoid it altogether. Responsible use comes from practice, not from staying on the sidelines.

What this means for you
Learn how to use it responsibly, experiment within UJA's guardrails, and build the capability together. Everyone is learning, and every question and experiment helps all of us get better.
